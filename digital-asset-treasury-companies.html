Digital asset treasury companies: the provider economics behind the strategy.

A digital asset treasury company is a public company that holds a material digital-asset position as part of its treasury strategy. Some manage that exposure internally. Others use external managers, advisers, staking providers, trading specialists or strategic-advisory firms. The economic structure of those provider relationships can materially affect recurring cost, governance flexibility and exit risk.

What is a digital asset treasury company?

The label usually refers to a company whose treasury strategy includes a material allocation to assets such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, AVAX, BNB, DOGE or other digital assets. The important distinction for provider research is not the token itself. It is whether a third party receives recurring fees, performance compensation, equity, warrants, governance rights or other consideration for helping manage, advise or operate the treasury strategy.

That is why two digital asset treasury companies with similar holdings can have very different provider economics.

Research question: who controls the treasury mandate, what is the provider paid, how long does the agreement run, what happens if the company wants to change course, and which rights survive termination?

Why provider agreements matter

A headline AUM fee is only one part of the economic stack. Public digital asset treasury agreements can combine recurring asset-based fees, performance fees, high-water marks, equity or warrant compensation, milestone awards, expense reimbursement, long contract terms, automatic renewals and termination-linked payments.

Core fields to benchmark

  • Provider role and decision authority
  • AUM / asset-based fee and exact denominator
  • Fee effective date versus first publicly observed accrual / invoice / payment
  • Performance fee, hurdle and high-water mark
  • Shares, warrants and other equity-linked compensation
  • Compensation waivers, suspension and revocation mechanics
  • Initial term and automatic renewal mechanics
  • Separate client-side and provider-side termination rights
  • Remaining-term fees, acceleration or liquidated damages
  • Related-party ownership and Board relationships
  • Consent, nomination or consultation rights
  • Amendment history and current contract version
  • Accrued / paid / unpaid fee status where publicly disclosed
  • Realized renegotiation, non-renewal, internalization or termination outcomes

Digital asset treasury companies do not all use the same provider model

Some public companies appoint a discretionary manager that can trade or stake digital assets. Others use a strategic adviser with a narrower mandate. Some combine multiple providers across asset management, staking, derivatives, custody coordination and financing. A useful benchmark therefore compares economic role before comparing price.

SUI Group is a clean example of the distinction: Galaxy is the discretionary asset manager, while Karatage is a strategic adviser whose compensation is also AUM-linked and whose agreement contains rights touching manager selection and replacement. Pineapple now shows an even broader stack: two original discretionary manager sleeves plus a later Board-approved adviser with asset-management and strategy-oversight scope. Lite Strategy adds a different normalization problem: its 1.75% GSR fee is equity-settled and sits beside separate warrants, so payment medium and dilution have to be captured separately from the rate. Axe adds a different one: a 1% base asset fee sits beside a 25% incentive fee above 7% and separate adviser warrants, while termination rights differ materially by party. AI Financial adds a provider-role transition problem: Kraken's discretionary manager mandate was not renewed, but the later Concrete agreement is described as vault infrastructure and deployment services rather than automatically as a successor discretionary manager. Upexi now supplies another direct role-transition control: its former GSR discretionary manager relationship ended in termination and arbitration, while the later Hivemind agreement is explicitly non-discretionary, pays 1.25% on issuer market capitalization in shares and sits beside a separate Blueprint staking layer. Solmate adds a separate date/currentness problem: its filed Strategic Advisor Agreement ties the Effective Date to the PIPE Closing Date, while issuer summaries use both Sep. 18 and Sep. 23 descriptions; the first annual SOL-AUM measurement therefore needs the same defined-term reconciliation before it is dollarized. Hyperliquid Strategies adds a financing-linked compensation problem: the adviser agreement remains active while a contractual 5% share + 15% warrant future-grant right is currently waived and can be reactivated for future financings through a disclosed notice mechanism.

Fee architecture and provider status can change independently

Public filings show that provider economics are not static. A relationship can be amended without ending, and one agreement family can terminate while another remains relevant. SkyAI moved from a $10 million prepaid first-year Sol Edge fee into a tiered Account Equity schedule; the Aug. 27 effective date has passed, but the reviewed public record does not yet show the first post-change monthly accrual or payment. Upexi adds a role-reclassification sequence: a terminated GSR manager agreement remains important as a realized outcome, while a later Hivemind advisory agreement is a different role family with a different denominator and payment medium rather than a like-for-like replacement. Solmate adds two distinct currentness questions: the Strategic Advisor Agreement has an effective-date disclosure conflict that matters to its first annual measurement, while the separate Pulsar agreement had payment and accrual paused in April and was then mutually terminated effective July 29 without further cost or obligation. Hyperliquid Strategies adds another: the five-year Rorschach agreement remains in place while its future-financing grant formula is waived unless revoked for future financings. AI Financial adds a clean renewal-state control: an announced provider became a 30-day discretionary manager and then rolled off through non-renewal while the treasury strategy itself continued. Pineapple adds a different currentness pattern: a new Treasury Reserve Policy adviser layer appears while later public evidence still directly identifies Monarq and does not establish whether Canary terminated. Axe adds a filing-to-filing currentness gap: March 2026 affirmatively repeats DNA's manager terms while the June filing preserves treasury activity without repeating DNA or the agreement. USBC changed its compensation architecture without removing the provider. Eightco changed rate and denominator. AlphaTON settled its DWF treasury-management relationship while separately continuing to disclose Alpha Sigma's 1% discretionary-manager agreement.

Exit economics can matter more than the annual fee

The practical cost of a provider often becomes visible only when the issuer tries to renegotiate, internalize or terminate the mandate. Long initial terms, narrow notice windows, consent rights, minimum payments and remaining-term compensation can make a seemingly ordinary annual fee expensive to change.

Lite Strategy / GSR adds a clean formula-based control: ordinary company exit is tied to ending the LTC strategy and the contract converts the remaining term into a present-value payment based on completed-year fees. SkyAI / Sol Edge provides an all-remaining-fee control. Axe / DNA provides a different control: a ten-year manager agreement where the public contract gives the client a cause-based exit but does not disclose an ordinary client no-cause path, while the manager can obtain a later no-cause path. AI Financial / Kraken sits at the other end of the spectrum: a 30-day mandate ended by simple non-renewal, with no disclosed settlement or remaining-term formula. CEA / 10X and Upexi / GSR show long-duration disputes. Eightco's amended Worldcoin Tower agreement provides an 85% remaining-fee formula. BNB Plus / Cypress shows a negotiated cash + preferred-equity exit followed by internalization.

Related-party structures need a broader benchmark

Some digital asset treasury companies use providers connected to directors, executives, strategic investors or other related parties. The related-party label is not itself a pricing conclusion. It is a reason to map the relationship precisely and compare both economics and governance rights against independent arrangements with comparable scope.

SUI Group identifies Karatage as co-founded by its Chairman and CIO, while the separate Galaxy agreement supplies the discretionary manager layer. Lite Strategy's separate GSR side letter created a provider-linked Board nomination right. SkyAI identifies both Sol Edge and Sol Markets as controlled by the brother of its CIO / director. Axe disclosed DNA leadership entering issuer Board / CIO roles at launch and a later CEO who had served as DNA President and Co-Founder. Solmate later disclosed Board and executive overlap with members of its strategic-adviser group and a separate related-party Pulsar relationship. Pineapple's May 2026 filing identifies affiliates of Innovating Capital among related-party treasury-placement counterparties. Hyperliquid Strategies provides a different governance control: Rorschach's separate Advisor Rights Agreement gives conditional Board nomination, Chairman and information rights that should be tracked independently from advisory compensation. BNB Plus disclosed its then-Chairman and CIO as Cypress affiliates before the July 2026 settlement and management changes. AlphaTON's 2026 annual report identifies its CIO / Chairman as CEO and CIO of Alpha Sigma Capital. AVAX One identifies Hivemind as controlled by the company's current chairman.

Public outcomes are part of the market evidence

A static fee table cannot show what happens when a provider relationship is stressed. Public companies have already disclosed fee resets, fee pauses, compensation waivers, non-renewals, unpaid accruals, negotiated exits, internalizations, settlements and litigation. Those outcomes help answer a more useful question: what economic and operational consequences have actually appeared when companies changed — or challenged — provider arrangements?

How Boards and finance teams can use this research

A digital asset treasury provider benchmark is most useful when there is a concrete decision trigger: a new manager agreement, an amendment, a recurring-fee review, related-party scrutiny, unpaid-fee accruals, a financing event, an activist challenge, a renewal decision, a compensation waiver/revocation decision, a renegotiation, internalization or termination.

A decision-ready record should answer

  • What is the provider actually being paid or accrued today?
  • Which agreement version is current?
  • Is a new fee schedule merely effective, or has a post-change accrual / invoice / payment actually been observed?
  • How comparable are the selected peer mandates?
  • How much compensation is cash versus equity-linked?
  • Which compensation rights are active, waived, suspended, revocable or terminated?
  • What is the earliest ordinary exit or non-renewal path for each party?
  • What compensation or rights survive a change of provider?
  • Which public peers have already reset fees, paused or waived compensation, not renewed, renegotiated, terminated, litigated or internalized?
